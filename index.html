        Beliefs and ceremonies

        Most written records of Native beliefs were made by outsiders, often missionaries and traders, and they are incomplete and sometimes wrong. Many of these traditions are alive today, and they differ from person to person. Religion is also an outside category, since many nations describe their teachings as a whole way of life. For anything that matters, ask the nation itself. Tribal websites and Wisconsin First Nations are good places to start.

        Archaeology

        What the ground has kept

        Every site here is more than 500 years old. Some still stand in the open as earthen mounds, a rebuilt stockade and carved rock. The rest was dug up or raised from a lake, and what survives is in museums and reports. Use the filter to separate the two, and select a marker to see a photo of the place or find and read what was found and what scholars think each place was for.

Many mounds and sites were destroyed by farming, roads and building, and some locations are kept private, so this map shows only a part of what exists. Please do not dig at or climb on mounds; they are burial places and are protected by Wisconsin law.

          People counted, 1840 to 2020

          The first census after Wisconsin became a territory counted about 31,000 settlers. Immigration from Germany, Scandinavia and Poland, then the growth of Milwaukee, took the total past 5.8 million.

          Dates before about 1600 are estimates and archaeologists revise them often. Wisconsin's eleven federally recognized tribes (the Menominee, Ho-Chunk, Forest County Potawatomi, Oneida, Stockbridge-Munsee and six Ojibwe bands) are still here, and their own histories run longer and deeper than any single timeline.
